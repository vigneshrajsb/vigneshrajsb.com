Humans + agents, for one person

Draft · 6 min read

In Humans + agents I wrote down what was working for our team, in five chapters: hands, context, guard, insight and loop. One line in the context chapter said to write down who you are and how you work once, where the agent reads it every session.

I wrote that line. My teammate Vahan kept going.

He built Ditto, a personal agent for work. It reads the places your work happens, keeps one list you can trust, remembers your people and projects, and learns from every correction you give it. I've been adopting it a piece at a time, keeping what fits. This post is what I'm learning from him, using the same five chapters.

The name

Ditto is the Pokémon that turns into a copy of whatever it looks at. This Ditto starts knowing nothing about you and slowly turns into a copy of how you work: how you write, what you'd prioritize, who to ask about what, and what you'd never put in a ticket.

That's the whole idea. A teammate who has watched you work for a few months.

A home, not a prompt

Matt Pocock has a short on the home repo pattern: put your skills, steering files and tooling in one repo, and point your agent at everything else from there. Ditto is that pattern, all grown up.

Your Ditto lives in a folder on your machine with three layers:

  • Core is the engine: skills, rules, scripts and templates. It's shared, and it never holds anyone's data.
  • Packs carry a team's or company's rules and sources, so the shared stuff isn't copied into every home.
  • You is everything about you: your profile, memory, voice, rules and logs. It has its own git and never leaves your machine.

Updating core never touches your layer. That split is what made adopting it easy. I didn't fork Vahan's agent. I took his core and kept my own changes in my own layer.

It also doesn't care much about the harness. Claude Code reads one entry file, Codex reads another, and both point at the same rules. Model choices are written as tiers (top, strong, standard, light), and each harness maps them to its own models. When the next tool shows up, the home moves with you.

Hands

Last time: you're the brain, it's the hands.

Ditto is built from modules you switch on one at a time: task sync, a daily brief, meeting prep and notes, memory, writing style, code work. Each module lists what it needs, like connectors, command-line tools and permissions, and a doctor script checks all of it before anything runs.

The one I'd start with is task sync. Asks are scattered across Slack, email, tickets and meetings, and a few always slip. Ditto pulls them into one list every morning, with the people waiting on you at the top.

Context

Last time: it knows what we tell it.

Ditto keeps memory the way a person does. It knows the people, the projects, the acronyms only your team uses, and what other people decided that affects you, with a link to where they said it. A weekly cleanup job tidies it up, the same way you'd go through your notes.

Two files go further than a memory. One is your voice, worked out from messages you've actually sent, so its writing sounds like you and not like an assistant. The other is a self model: how you decide, what you do yourself and what you hand off, and what you never do. When Ditto has to make a call, it asks what you would do. That file only changes when you approve it.

Guard

Last time: it does exactly what we let it.

Every kind of action Ditto takes has an explicit list: tools it may use without asking, and tools it may never use, however the request is worded. What it's allowed to do on its own lives in one file in your profile, as a list of standing OKs. Your own rules can tighten the core rules, but never loosen the safety ones.

Today that list is short on purpose. It starts out like a new hire on their first week.

Insight

Last time: if you can't see it, you can't steer it.

Ditto has a small menu bar app, the Hub. It shows what's waiting on you, a feed of what Ditto heard across your channels, and how each scheduled run went. Every run ends with a short report: what it did, what it healed on its own (a broken step it diagnosed and fixed), and what it skipped because only you could do it.

That last part is my favorite. Unattended work never waits on you in silence. It finishes what it can and tells you the one thing it needs.

Loop

Last time: our transcripts are a coach.

Ditto turns that into a habit. Every message you send is checked for a correction. "Too long." "Ask Pat, not the whole channel." "Never put that in a ticket." Each one becomes a rule in the right file, in the same turn, and Ditto tells you in one line what it kept and where. You never have to say "remember this."

Once a week it reviews itself: what you corrected, what you ignored, what you redid. It proposes changes to its rules and to your self model, and you approve them or don't.

It's the loop from the last post, but for one person, every day.

Where this is going

Ditto doesn't exist to write drafts forever. The goal is an agent that does the work: sends the Slack reply, files the ticket, opens the PR, follows up when someone goes quiet.

The way it gets there is what I like most. Ditto keeps a ledger of every kind of action, and of how often you accept its output as is. Once a kind of action has enough history and you've stopped changing it, Ditto proposes a new standing OK for that kind, and only that kind. You grant it, and it stops asking.

That's how trust works with people, too. Nobody gets the keys on day one. You earn them one kind of task at a time, by being right in the same way enough times in a row.

What I'm taking from Vahan

  • Build the home, not the prompt. A folder you own outlives every model and every harness.
  • Corrections are the product. Every "too long" is a free lesson, if something writes it down.
  • Autonomy is earned per action. Not all at once, and not by default.

The last post ended by asking you to hand over the whole job around one task. This one asks a smaller thing. The next time you correct an agent, notice whether it'll remember tomorrow. If it won't, that's where your home starts.

Thanks, Vahan.
